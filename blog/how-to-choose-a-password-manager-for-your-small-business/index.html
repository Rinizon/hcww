How to Choose a Password Manager for Your Small Business

Published October 15, 2026

A business password manager gives employees a safer way to create, store, and share account credentials. The right choice should improve security while making everyday sign-ins easier to manage.

Reused passwords, shared spreadsheets, and credentials sent through email can turn one compromised account into a much larger business problem. A password manager replaces those habits with encrypted storage, unique passwords, and controlled sharing. Choosing one is less about finding the longest feature list and more about selecting a dependable tool your team will actually use.

Start with a business plan, not a personal account

Personal password managers can protect one person's accounts, but a business needs centralized administration. Look for a plan that lets an owner or administrator add and remove users, assign access, review security settings, and recover business access when an employee leaves. Each employee should have an individual account rather than sharing one master login. This keeps activity attributable and makes access changes much cleaner.

Confirm how the provider protects your vault

A credible provider should clearly explain how vault data is encrypted, whether encryption happens before data leaves a device, and what information the company itself can access. Review its security documentation, independent audits, breach history, and response practices. No product can promise that an incident will never happen, but the design should limit what an attacker or even the provider can learn from stored vaults.

Require multifactor authentication

The password manager becomes a doorway to many important accounts, so protect it with more than a master password. Choose a product that allows administrators to require multifactor authentication for every user. Security keys or authenticator apps are generally stronger choices than text messages. Employees also need a documented way to store recovery codes without putting those codes inside the vault they are meant to recover.

Evaluate password sharing and permissions

Shared credentials are sometimes unavoidable, but everyone does not need access to everything. A useful business password manager organizes credentials into collections, groups, or shared vaults and lets administrators grant access based on job responsibilities. It should be simple to share a credential without exposing it in chat or email. Check whether the tool records important administrative changes and allows access to be removed promptly.

Check support for the devices your team uses

The manager should work on the browsers, computers, and mobile devices employees use for business. Test its browser extension, password generator, autofill, and mobile experience before committing the whole company. Awkward tools encourage workarounds. A short pilot with a few employees can reveal compatibility problems and training needs while the change is still easy to adjust.

Plan for account recovery without weakening security

Forgotten master passwords and lost devices will happen. Compare the recovery options available to administrators and understand exactly what they can and cannot restore. Identify at least two trusted administrators so one unavailable person does not lock the business out. Document emergency access steps, protect administrator accounts especially well, and test the process before an urgent situation forces you to learn it under pressure.

Make onboarding and offboarding straightforward

A new employee should receive an individual vault, multifactor authentication instructions, and access only to the credentials needed for the role. When someone leaves, administrators should be able to suspend the account, transfer appropriate business records, remove trusted devices, and rotate sensitive shared passwords. Add these actions to your new-employee IT onboarding checklist and employee IT offboarding checklist so the process is consistent.

Compare total cost, not just the monthly price

Review per-user pricing, minimum seat requirements, renewal terms, support levels, and charges for features such as single sign-on, advanced reports, or directory integration. Then consider the time required to deploy and support the tool. Paying slightly more for clear administration and responsive support may cost less than repeatedly untangling a cheaper product that employees avoid.

Create a practical rollout plan

Begin with administrators and a small pilot group. Define vault structure, naming rules, sharing standards, recovery procedures, and which accounts must be moved first. Train employees to create unique generated passwords, recognize suspicious sign-in prompts, and report unexpected activity. Avoid trying to migrate every personal and business login in one afternoon; prioritize email, financial systems, cloud services, website tools, and other high-impact accounts.

Review password health regularly

Deployment is the beginning, not the finish. Use the product's reporting tools to find reused, weak, or exposed passwords without turning the process into employee surveillance. Review administrators, shared collections, inactive users, recovery options, and connected devices on a recurring schedule. Include these checks in your broader small-business cybersecurity plan so ownership and follow-up are clear.

What should a small business password manager include?

At minimum, look for strong encryption, individual employee accounts, required multifactor authentication, secure sharing, role-based permissions, password generation, reliable browser and mobile apps, administrator recovery, and timely support. Reporting, security-key support, and integration with existing identity systems can become more important as the team grows. The best fit is the one that covers your real risks and remains understandable enough to use every day.

A password manager works best as part of a complete account-security routine, not as a stand-alone purchase.
