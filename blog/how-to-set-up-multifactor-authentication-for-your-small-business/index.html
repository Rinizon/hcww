How to Set Up Multifactor Authentication for Your Small Business

Published October 19, 2026

Multifactor authentication adds a second check beyond a password and can stop many account takeovers. A thoughtful rollout protects the business without making daily work unnecessarily difficult.

Passwords are routinely stolen through phishing, reused across services, or exposed in data breaches. Multifactor authentication, often called MFA or two-factor authentication, requires another form of proof before a person can sign in. It is one of the most useful security improvements a small business can make, but coverage, recovery, and employee support matter just as much as switching the feature on.

What is multifactor authentication?

Authentication factors usually fall into three groups: something you know, such as a password; something you have, such as a phone or security key; and something you are, such as a fingerprint. MFA combines factors from different groups. A password plus a second password is not true MFA, while a password plus a code from an authenticator app is. If an attacker learns the password, the second factor can still block the sign-in.

Start with the accounts that could cause the most damage

Begin with email because it is often used to reset other passwords. Then protect banking, payroll, accounting, cloud storage, domain registration, website administration, social media, remote access, and any account with administrator privileges. Make a simple list of important services, their owners, current MFA status, and available methods. This inventory also belongs in your broader small-business cybersecurity plan.

Choose the strongest practical MFA method

Security keys and passkeys are highly resistant to phishing because they verify the real website before approving a sign-in. Authenticator apps that generate time-based codes or send well-designed approval prompts are another solid option. Text-message codes are better than a password alone, but phone-number theft and intercepted messages make them a weaker choice. Email codes offer limited protection when the email account itself is being attacked. Use the strongest method each service and workflow can reliably support.

Secure administrators before rolling out to everyone

Owners, managers, IT providers, and anyone with administrative access should go first. These accounts can often change settings, create users, view sensitive records, or turn MFA off for others. Give each administrator an individual account, avoid shared admin credentials, and register at least two approved factors where the service permits it. Protect the password itself with a business password manager.

Run a small pilot before company-wide deployment

Test MFA with a few employees who use different devices and job workflows. Confirm that desktop, mobile, remote-work, and shared-workstation sign-ins behave as expected. Record confusing screens, unsupported applications, and the steps needed to replace a phone. A pilot gives you time to fix gaps and create clear instructions before a deadline affects the entire team.

Prepare employees for the change

Explain why MFA is being required, which accounts are included, which methods are approved, and when enrollment must be completed. Short instructions with screenshots are more useful than a long policy. Employees should know never to approve an unexpected prompt and to report repeated requests immediately; a stream of prompts may mean someone already has the password and is trying to wear the user down.

Plan recovery before anyone loses a device

Every protected account needs a recovery path. Store one-time recovery codes in a secure, access-controlled location, not on the same phone used for authentication. For critical services, register a backup security key and keep it somewhere physically safe. Document who may verify an employee's identity, reset MFA, and review the account after recovery. At least two trusted people should understand the process so one absence does not lock out the business.

Avoid exceptions that quietly become permanent

Older applications, shared accounts, service accounts, and vendor access can complicate enforcement. Do not simply exempt them and forget them. Record each exception, why it is needed, who owns it, what protection compensates for the risk, and when it will be reviewed. Replace shared logins with named accounts where possible and ask vendors whether they support modern sign-in methods.

Add MFA to onboarding and offboarding

During onboarding, enroll approved factors, provide recovery guidance, and confirm access before the employee begins relying on the account. During offboarding, disable the user's sessions and accounts, remove registered devices and keys, and transfer business-owned records. Use the existing IT onboarding checklist and IT offboarding checklist to make these steps repeatable.

Review MFA coverage regularly

Check quarterly—or after major staffing or system changes—that active users remain enrolled and important services still enforce MFA. Review administrator roles, backup factors, recovery contacts, inactive accounts, and approved exceptions. Also look for new applications that employees adopted since the last review. MFA is most effective when it is a maintained business standard rather than a one-time project.

What is the best MFA for a small business?

For the most sensitive and administrative accounts, phishing-resistant security keys or passkeys are the strongest practical choice. Authenticator apps are a dependable option for many everyday services, while text messages can serve as a temporary fallback when stronger methods are unavailable. The best rollout prioritizes complete coverage, documented recovery, and methods employees can use consistently.
