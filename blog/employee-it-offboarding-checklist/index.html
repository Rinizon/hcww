Employee IT Offboarding Checklist

Published September 21, 2026

When an employee leaves, a small business needs to protect company information without losing the files, conversations, and customer history the rest of the team still needs. This checklist covers the essential IT offboarding steps.

IT offboarding is the process of removing a departing worker's access, recovering company equipment, and transferring business data to the right people. A rushed or informal process can leave active accounts, forgotten subscriptions, and important files behind. A documented checklist makes the transition safer and easier to verify.

Decide who owns the offboarding process

Choose one person to coordinate the checklist, even if a manager, payroll provider, and IT support partner each handle different tasks. Record the employee's final working time, whether access should end immediately or at the close of the last day, and who is authorized to approve exceptions. For an unexpected or high-risk departure, involve the appropriate business, human-resources, and legal advisers before taking action.

Build an inventory of accounts and access

Start with the access assigned during onboarding, then check for anything added later. Include business email, cloud storage, accounting, customer relationship management, payroll, website administration, social media, remote access, password managers, collaboration tools, software licenses, door codes, and alarm systems. Review both individual accounts and shared credentials the employee knew.

Disable access at the agreed time

Suspend or disable the employee's accounts according to the departure plan. Revoke active sessions, application passwords, access tokens, virtual private network access, and registered multifactor authentication methods. Remove the person from email groups, shared drives, team channels, and administrative roles. Disabling an account first is usually safer than immediately deleting it because the business may still need its data.

Change shared passwords and codes

Reset any shared password, recovery code, wireless credential, door code, or vendor login the employee could use. Store the replacement in the company's approved password manager and tell only the people who still need it. This is also a good time to replace shared accounts with named user accounts when the service supports them.

Preserve email and business records

Before deleting anything, preserve the records the company is required or reasonably expected to keep. Transfer ownership of documents, calendars, forms, dashboards, and automation workflows. Give a manager access to necessary business correspondence using the platform's supported delegation or retention features instead of asking for the departing employee's password.

Handle incoming email carefully

Decide whether messages should be forwarded, monitored by an authorized person, or answered with a temporary notice that directs customers to a current contact. Avoid an indefinite forwarding rule that no one reviews. Set an owner and an end date, then document when the old address can be retired under the company's retention policy.

Recover company devices and equipment

Collect computers, phones, tablets, security keys, storage devices, chargers, docking stations, access cards, and any other assigned property. Compare returned items with the equipment inventory and record their condition. For a remote employee, arrange tracked shipping and provide clear packing instructions rather than relying on an informal return plan.

Protect data on returned or missing devices

Once a device is back, preserve any required business data and follow a documented process to erase it before reassignment or disposal. Do not simply delete the old user folder and hand the computer to someone else. If equipment is not returned, use approved device-management tools to lock or erase company data when appropriate, and change any credentials that may have been stored on it.

Transfer responsibilities and ownership

Reassign open support requests, customer contacts, recurring reports, domain names, website services, advertising accounts, cloud resources, and vendor relationships. Check scheduled automations and billing notifications that may still depend on the old email address. Critical services should be owned by the business, not by one employee's personal email account or payment method.

Recover licenses and update support records

Remove or reassign paid licenses after the required data is secured. Update asset, account, and support documentation so it reflects the new owner and current access. Keeping these records accurate prevents unnecessary subscription costs and makes the next audit, onboarding, or offboarding much faster.

Verify that offboarding is complete

Have a second person review the checklist. Confirm that the former employee cannot sign in, that important records remain available, that devices are accounted for, and that customers have a working contact. Keep a dated record of completed steps and approvals without storing passwords in the checklist.

Use every departure to improve the process

If the team discovers an unknown account, missing device, or workflow owned by a personal address, add it to the standard process. Offboarding is easier when it begins with good onboarding: individual accounts, limited permissions, accurate equipment records, and business-owned services all reduce uncertainty when someone's role changes.
