New-Employee IT Onboarding Checklist

Published September 16, 2026

A smooth first day starts before a new employee arrives. This checklist helps a small business prepare the right computer, accounts, access, security, and support without giving away more access than the job requires.

Technology delays can turn a new hire's first morning into hours of password resets, missing software, and improvised permissions. A repeatable IT onboarding process gives the employee what they need to contribute while protecting company information from avoidable mistakes and unnecessary access.

Start with the role, not the person

Define the technology a role needs before creating accounts. List the applications, shared folders, email groups, printers, phone systems, and business data used by someone doing that work. A role-based checklist makes future onboarding faster and reduces the chance that permissions are copied from an employee with different responsibilities.

Choose and prepare the computer early

Confirm whether the employee needs a desktop, laptop, docking station, monitors, headset, or other equipment. Order hardware with enough lead time for setup. Before the start date, install operating-system updates, approved applications, security tools, device encryption, and remote-support software. Label the device and record its serial number, assigned user, warranty, and purchase date in an equipment inventory.

Create individual accounts

Give each employee their own business email address and named account for every system they use. Shared logins make it difficult to control access, investigate problems, or remove one person's permissions later. Use a consistent naming standard and avoid reusing an old employee's account, even when the replacement has the same role.

Grant only the access the job requires

Apply the principle of least privilege: start with the minimum access needed for the employee's responsibilities, then add permissions when there is a clear business need. Separate everyday accounts from administrative accounts and document who approved access to sensitive systems such as payroll, accounting, customer records, or backups.

Turn on essential security controls

Require multifactor authentication for email, cloud applications, and remote access. Make sure the employee uses a company-approved password manager rather than saving passwords in notes or browsers without oversight. Confirm that antivirus or endpoint protection is active, automatic updates are enabled, and the device can be locked or erased if it is lost.

Set up communication and shared resources

Add the employee to the correct calendars, distribution lists, collaboration channels, shared drives, and phone queues. Check that licenses have been assigned and that the employee can reach the files and tools needed for their first assignments. If the role is remote or hybrid, test the internet connection, virtual private network, calling tools, and meeting equipment before the first important meeting.

Explain the company's technology rules

Review expectations for acceptable use, personal devices, file storage, software installation, remote work, and handling confidential information. Show the employee how to recognize suspicious email and where to report it. Short, practical guidance is more useful than handing over a long policy without discussing how it applies to daily work.

Test the setup before the first day

Sign in to the prepared device with a temporary onboarding account or use an approved testing process to confirm that software launches, licenses work, and required services are reachable. Do not ask for or retain the employee's password. Instead, provide a secure account-activation process that requires the employee to create a private password and enroll in multifactor authentication.

Give the employee a clear support path

Tell the new hire how to request help, what information to include, and whom to contact for urgent problems. Schedule a quick check-in after the first few days to catch missing permissions or equipment issues. Record any changes so the role checklist improves for the next hire.

Connect onboarding with offboarding

Every account, device, license, and permission created during onboarding should be easy to find when an employee changes roles or leaves. Maintain one authoritative checklist and assign responsibility for notifying IT about staffing changes. Good records make it easier to recover equipment, preserve business data, remove access promptly, and reuse paid licenses.

A repeatable checklist makes growth easier

Small businesses do not need a complicated onboarding system. They need a consistent one with clear ownership. Preparing the device, using individual accounts, limiting access, enabling security controls, and testing the setup creates a more productive first day and a safer long-term technology environment.
